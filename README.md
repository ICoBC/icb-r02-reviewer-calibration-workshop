# Reviewer Calibration Workshop

*Annual calibration of Lead Reviewers on shared cases.*

## About this course

This workshop is for ICoBC Lead Reviewers (N2). Each year, Lead Reviewers who assess issuers and partner engagements come together to check that they apply the same criteria in the same way. You practise on shared cases, compare your scores, find out why you differ, agree on interpretations and record them in the shared interpretation notes.

The course is aligned with the ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill QUAL "Quality assurance and accreditation" (VRITE pillar: Rigor), level L4 "Assure", stage A "Ability". At this level, a person designs and leads internal quality assurance and conducts independent reviews of other issuers. The Ability stage means you practise a capability on realistic tasks in a controlled setting. Here the controlled setting is a set of shared cases.

**Prerequisite:** ICB-R01 Global Quality Seal: Lead Reviewer Training.

**Learning outcomes.** After this workshop you can:

1. Score shared cases independently and compare results.
2. Identify sources of disagreement and agree interpretations.
3. Update the shared interpretation notes.

**Workload.** The workshop has 4 hours of notional learning. This file covers the self-paced part. The live elements (the group comparison and the agreement discussion) are described in facilitator notes.

**Assessment.** You take part in the workshop with scored cases, and your scores must agree within the set tolerance. A person assesses your final task with the rubric in this file. The file does not grade your work.

**Badge.** "Reviewer Calibration Workshop (Ability, L4)", alignment code ICSF-QUAL-L4-A. The badge evidence is your calibration record.

## Independent scoring

### Why calibration needs independent scores

A seal or badge decision should not depend on which reviewer received the file. Calibration checks this. Each Lead Reviewer scores the same cases alone, before any discussion. Independence matters because once reviewers talk, they tend to move towards the first strong opinion in the room. You would then measure social agreement, not shared understanding of the criteria.

Independent scoring has three rules:

1. **Score before you talk.** Do not discuss a case with other reviewers until all scores are submitted.
2. **Score from the evidence.** Use only what is in the case file. If something is missing, record "evidence missing" and score what you can see.
3. **Write a reason for every score.** One or two sentences that point to the evidence. A score without a reason cannot be compared later.

The scoring scale for the workshop is provided by your facilitator with the case pack. Use that scale exactly as written. Do not add half points or your own bands.

### Working with criteria that are still emerging

The VRITE criteria and the Global Quality Seal are still in an Emerging state. Indicators are not yet published, and internal documents expand the acronym in different ways. This affects how you score. You may meet a criterion whose wording allows more than one reading.

When this happens:

- Score using the wording in the case pack for this workshop.
- Note in your reason which reading you used.
- Do not guess what the final indicators will say.

A recorded reading is valuable data. In later modules, those notes help the group find where wording needs more detail.

### Recording your scores

Use the calibration record from your facilitator. For every case and criterion, write:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Case | Case number |
| Criterion | Criterion name as written in the case pack |
| Score | A value from the scale |
| Reason | One or two sentences pointing to evidence |
| Reading used | Only if the wording allowed more than one reading |

Keep your first scores. If you change your mind later, add a new line and keep the original. The comparison in the next module depends on your first, independent judgement.

**Worked example: A partner engagement case**

An ICoBC Lead Reviewer, Amara, receives Case 1: a regional training provider that applies to join as a partner issuer. The case file contains a description of its assessment process, two sample badge descriptions and an internal moderation log.

For the criterion on assessment rigour, Amara reads the moderation log. It shows that a second assessor checked a sample of learner work in the last cycle, but the log does not say how the sample was chosen. Amara scores the criterion 3 on the scale (illustrative value) and writes: "Second assessor check is documented, sample selection method is not shown. Scored on the documented check only. Reading used: 'moderation' taken to mean any second-assessor check, not only random sampling."

Notice what Amara did. She scored from the evidence, named the missing item, stated her reading and kept the reason short. Another reviewer can now see exactly why she scored as she did, even if they would choose a different number.

**Practice activity: Score Case 1 independently**

Your facilitator sends you Case 1 and the scoring scale.

1. Read the case file once without scoring.
2. Score each criterion in the case pack.
3. Write a reason for every score, and record the reading used wherever the wording was open.
4. Submit your scores to the facilitator before the live session. Do not discuss the case with anyone.

## Comparison

### From scores to a comparison table

After submission, the facilitator collects all scores and builds a comparison table. The table shows, for each case and criterion, every reviewer's score side by side, together with the spread. The spread is the difference between the highest and the lowest score on that criterion.

You compare scores against the tolerance set for the workshop. The tolerance is the largest spread that counts as agreement. If the spread is within tolerance, the criterion is calibrated for that case. If the spread is larger, the criterion needs discussion.

<!-- VERIFY: confirm how the tolerance is expressed (for example, maximum spread in scale points) in the facilitator's case pack. -->

Spread is only one view. Also look at:

- **Direction.** Does one reviewer score higher than the rest on most criteria? This may point to a personal tendency to be lenient or strict.
- **Clusters.** Do reviewers split into two groups on the same criterion? This often points to two readings of the wording.
- **Outliers.** Does one score sit far from the others? Check the reason first. The reviewer may have seen evidence the others missed.

### Reading a spread chart

The facilitator may share a chart of score spread. A spread chart shows each reviewer's score as a dot on a line. When the dots sit close together, the group agrees. When they are far apart, the group needs to talk.

Calibration aims to reduce the spread over time. A useful way to see the effect is to compare the spread before and after calibration discussion. The next chart shows the idea with illustrative values.

### Looking for patterns, not people

Calibration compares judgements, not people. When you read the comparison table, ask "Where do we differ, and why?" and avoid "Who is wrong?" A different score is not a mistake until the reasons show that the reviewer misread the evidence or the criterion.

Use the written reasons to sort differences into three groups:

1. **Evidence differences.** One reviewer saw or missed a piece of evidence.
2. **Wording differences.** Reviewers read the criterion in different ways.
3. **Standard differences.** Reviewers apply a different level of strictness.

You will use these groups in the next module. For now, mark each criterion where the spread is above tolerance and write a first guess at the group.

**Worked example: Comparing four reviewers**

In an ICoBC review of a partner engagement, four Lead Reviewers score Case 1. On the criterion for assessment rigour, the scores (illustrative) are 3, 3, 4 and 1 on a scale of 1 to 5. The tolerance is a spread of 1 point (illustrative).

The spread is 3 points, which is above tolerance. The facilitator opens the reasons. Three reviewers wrote that a second-assessor check was documented. The reviewer who scored 1 wrote: "No random sampling shown, so no valid moderation." The reasons show a wording difference: "moderation" was read as any second check by three reviewers and as random sampling by one.

The reviewer with the score of 1 was not careless. She read the criterion more strictly. The group now has a precise question to resolve in the agreement step: what does "moderation" require?

**Practice activity: Build your comparison**

Your facilitator shares an anonymised comparison table for Case 1.

1. Mark every criterion where the spread is above tolerance.
2. For each marked criterion, read the reasons and note whether the cause looks like an evidence, wording or standard difference.
3. Add your notes to the comparison section of your calibration record.
4. Keep your own first score visible next to the group scores.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

Anonymise the table before sharing, or use reviewer codes. Display the tolerance clearly. In the live session, spend about 20 minutes on the table and do not yet resolve any disagreement. Ask each reviewer to read out one reason that differs from their own score and say what they learned. Stop any attempt to decide the correct reading, as that belongs to Module 3.

## Agreement

### Finding the source of each disagreement

In the live session, the group works through every criterion where the spread is above tolerance. For each one, the group answers three questions in order:

1. **What did each reviewer see?** Check that all reviewers worked from the same evidence.
2. **What did each reviewer read?** Compare how each person understood the criterion wording.
3. **What standard did each reviewer apply?** Ask what level of detail or proof the reviewer expected.

Take these in order. Many disagreements end at question 1 because one reviewer missed a page. Wording and standard questions need more time.

Sorting each difference into a source makes the next step clear:

| Source | Typical resolution |
|---|---|
| Evidence | Agree which evidence is in scope and re-score |
| Wording | Agree one reading and write it down |
| Standard | Agree what level of proof the criterion needs |

### Agreeing an interpretation

An agreed interpretation is a short statement of how reviewers will read a criterion when they meet similar evidence. It should be:

- **Specific.** It names what counts and what does not.
- **Testable.** Another reviewer can apply it to a new case.
- **Short.** One to three sentences.

Example form: "For criterion X, 'moderation' means a documented check of assessed work by a second qualified person. A random selection method is [required / not required]."

The group agrees by discussion until reviewers can accept the statement. If they cannot reach agreement in the session, record the open question. Do not force a vote to close it. An open question that is recorded honestly is better than a false agreement.

Because the VRITE indicators are still emerging, some interpretations will be provisional. Mark these as provisional and give a reason. Do not present a provisional reading as final.

### Re-scoring after agreement

Once the group has agreed an interpretation, each reviewer re-scores the affected criterion using the agreed reading. Keep the original score in the record and add the new one. The change between the two shows what calibration achieved.

Check the new spread against the tolerance. If it is within tolerance, the criterion is calibrated for this case. If it is not, the interpretation needs more precision, or the difference comes from a source you have not yet found. Return to the three questions.

Do not re-score to match the group without understanding why. If you disagree with the agreed reading, say so in the session and record your concern. Calibration works only when reviewers state their real views.

**Worked example: Agreeing what "moderation" means**

In an ICoBC review of a partner engagement, the four Lead Reviewers from the previous module meet to resolve the spread on assessment rigour. The facilitator asks the three questions.

On evidence: all four saw the same moderation log. No difference.

On reading: three read "moderation" as any documented second-assessor check. One read it as random sampling.

On standard: the group discusses what level of proof the criterion needs. They agree that the purpose of moderation is to check that assessors judge consistently, and that a documented second-assessor check serves that purpose. They also agree that the sample selection method should be recorded.

The group writes: "Moderation means a documented check of assessed work by a second qualified person. Sample selection should be described. Where selection is not described, score on the documented check and note the gap." They mark this as provisional because the indicators are not yet published. All four re-score, and the new scores (illustrative) are 3, 3, 3 and 2. The spread is 1, within tolerance.

**Practice activity: Draft an agreed interpretation**

Using one criterion from Case 1 where your group scores were above tolerance:

1. Identify the source of the difference: evidence, wording or standard.
2. Draft an interpretation statement of one to three sentences that is specific and testable.
3. Mark it as final or provisional, and give a reason.
4. Re-score the criterion using your draft, keeping your first score in the record.
5. Bring your draft to the live session for group discussion.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

Allow about 60 minutes in the live session for agreement. Take criteria in order of spread, largest first. For each, run the three questions in sequence and do not move to the next criterion until the group has either agreed a statement or recorded an open question. Write each statement on a shared screen so all reviewers see the same text. Watch for pressure on reviewers with minority views, and invite them to state concerns. Record every provisional reading and the reason for it. Do not announce which reading is "correct" from authority; the aim is shared understanding. If a wording problem cannot be resolved, note it for the criteria owners to consider.

</details>

## Interpretation notes

### What the shared interpretation notes are

The shared interpretation notes are a living document in which Lead Reviewers keep agreed readings of criteria. They help a reviewer who was not in the room apply the criteria in the same way. They also show new reviewers how the group has resolved difficult cases.

Each entry in the notes should be short and follow the same template. A consistent template makes entries easy to find, compare and update.

The template has these fields:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|

### Writing and updating an entry

Write the entry soon after the session, while the discussion is fresh. Use plain words, and avoid abbreviations that new reviewers may not know. Check that the entry says what the group agreed, not what you think it should have agreed.

When you update an existing entry:

1. **Keep the history.** Do not overwrite the earlier text. Add a dated change line that says what changed and why.
2. **Check for conflicts.** Read related entries. If your new reading conflicts with an older one, flag both and ask the group to decide.
3. **Mark the status.** If the criteria are still emerging, keep the entry provisional until the criteria owners confirm the wording.
4. **Ask for review.** A second Lead Reviewer who attended the session checks that the entry matches what was agreed.

The notes record how ICoBC reviewers read the wording they were given. They do not replace the criteria or the indicators. When indicators are published, reviewers check each entry against them and update or retire the entry where needed.

### Keeping the notes usable

Notes lose value when they grow without care. Once a year, at the calibration workshop, the group should:

- Remove entries that no longer apply.
- Merge entries that say the same thing.
- Check provisional entries and decide whether they can become final.
- Test a few entries on a new case, to see if reviewers still reach comparable scores.

An entry that reviewers cannot apply consistently needs rewriting, even if it sounds correct.

**Worked example: Updating the entry on moderation**

After the agreement session for Case 1, one Lead Reviewer, Jonas, writes the entry for "moderation" in an ICoBC partner engagement review.

- Criterion: Assessment rigour.
- Question: Does "moderation" require random sampling of assessed work?
- Agreed interpretation: Moderation means a documented check of assessed work by a second qualified person. Sample selection should be described. Where selection is not described, score on the documented check and note the gap.
- Status: Provisional. Indicators are not yet published.
- Example: Meets: a log that shows a second assessor checked ten samples and describes how they were chosen. Does not meet: a statement that "assessments are checked" with no log.
- Source case: Case 1, calibration workshop, 2026.
- Open concerns: One reviewer holds that random sampling should be required.
- Review date: next annual workshop.

Jonas asks a second reviewer who attended to check the entry. She confirms that it matches the discussion. Jonas also finds an older entry on "internal verification" that uses a similar idea, and flags both entries for the group to align.

**Practice activity: Write an interpretation notes entry**

Using the interpretation statement you drafted in the previous module:

1. Complete every field of the template.
2. Write one example of evidence that meets the reading and one that does not.
3. Check the other entries your facilitator provides, and flag any conflict.
4. Ask a peer who attended the session to check your entry against what was agreed.
5. Add the final entry to your calibration record.

## Assessment and badge

### The task

The final task is the calibration record. It brings together your work from all four modules. A trained assessor reviews it using the rubric below. The file does not grade your work.

**Your calibration record contains:**

1. **Independent scores.** Your first scores and reasons for each shared case, with any readings used.
2. **Comparison.** Your marked comparison table, with the source of each difference above tolerance.
3. **Agreement.** The agreed interpretations from the live session, your re-scores and any concerns you recorded.
4. **Interpretation notes.** Your completed entry in the template, with an example and the peer check.
5. **Reflection.** A short note of up to 200 words on what you will do differently in your next review.

Your record must show that your scores agree within the set tolerance after calibration.

### Rubric

| Criterion | Meets the standard | Needs further work |
|---|---|---|
| Independent scoring | Every score has a reason that cites evidence; first scores are kept; readings used are recorded | Scores lack reasons, evidence is assumed, or first scores are overwritten |
| Comparison | Spread is calculated correctly against tolerance; sources of difference are sorted into evidence, wording and standard | Spread is wrongly calculated, or differences are attributed to people |
| Agreement | Interpretations are specific, testable and short; open questions and concerns are recorded honestly; re-scores are within tolerance | Statements are vague, open questions are hidden, or re-scores match the group without explanation |
| Interpretation notes | All template fields are complete; example shows meets and does not meet; status and reason are stated; peer check is recorded | Fields are missing, no example is given, or provisional status is not explained |
| Professional practice | Provisional readings are not presented as final; no claims go beyond the evidence | Provisional readings are presented as final, or the record claims more than the evidence supports |

**Pass rule.** The assessor judges that every criterion meets the standard, and that your post-calibration scores agree within the set tolerance. If a criterion needs further work, you receive written feedback and may resubmit that part.

**Badge evidence.** You submit your calibration record. On a pass, you receive the badge "Reviewer Calibration Workshop (Ability, L4)", alignment code ICSF-QUAL-L4-A.

> The badge shows that you completed this workshop and met its assessment. It does not prove competence in any job role.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

Assessors use the rubric as written and compare each record with the case pack and the agreed tolerance. They give written feedback for every criterion marked "needs further work". A second assessor checks a sample of records to keep assessment consistent. Reviewers have one resubmission window, set by the facilitator. Store records according to ICoBC's data handling rules.
